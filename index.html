The Affordability Gap: How Housing Became Out of Reach

Forty years of data reveal how the American dream of homeownership became financially impossible for millions — and why today's crisis is unlike anything we've seen before.

By Ananya Mehta Data: FRED / Federal Reserve 1984 – 2024

The Story

A benchmark broken for a generation

For decades, financial advisors have offered a simple rule of thumb: a home should cost no more than three times your annual income. It was a reasonable norm for most of the 20th century. Then, quietly at first and then all at once, it stopped being true.

Using data from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, I traced every quarter from 1984 to 2024 — watching home prices, household incomes, and mortgage rates move together and apart. What emerged is a picture of a housing market that has fundamentally changed, not just cyclically but structurally.

"The median U.S. home now costs more than six times the median annual household income — twice what conventional affordability guidelines say it should."

This project asks three questions: How far has the price-to-income ratio drifted from its historical norm? When does the monthly cost of a mortgage become genuinely unaffordable? And is today's crisis the same kind of problem as the 2008 housing bubble — or something structurally different?

Finding One

The 3× rule hasn't held for thirty years

The price-to-income ratio first crossed the 3× benchmark in the early 1990s and has barely looked back. By the mid-2000s it was approaching 5×. After the 2008 crash it pulled back — but only to around 4×, never returning to the historical norm. Then came the pandemic.

Between 2020 and 2022, remote work untethered millions of workers from expensive coastal cities. Demand surged. Supply couldn't keep up. Home prices rose faster in two years than they had in the previous decade.

Chart 1 — Price-to-Income Ratio, 1984–2024

The dashed red line marks the conventional 3× affordability benchmark. The ratio has been above it almost continuously since the early 1990s.

The most striking aspect of this chart isn't the peaks — it's the floors. After every correction, the ratio stabilized at a higher baseline than before. The 1984 baseline of ~3.5× gave way to a post-2008 floor of ~4×, which has since given way to a post-2020 floor settling above 5×.

Finding Two

Three eras of unaffordability — each with a different cause

The price-to-income ratio is a useful long-run measure, but it doesn't capture what a buyer actually experiences each month. For that, I calculated the monthly mortgage payment on the median home each quarter, assuming a 20% down payment and a 30-year fixed loan at the prevailing rate — then measured that payment as a share of monthly income.

Mortgage lenders use 28% as their threshold for "cost-burdened."

Chart 2 — Monthly Payment Burden, 1984–2024

The dashed red line marks the 28% cost-burden threshold. The shaded area shows the full payment burden each quarter.

Early 1980s — The Rate Era

Mortgage rates reached 13–14%. Even with modest home prices by modern standards, the monthly payment consumed well over 28% of income. The affordability problem was almost entirely a rate problem.

2005–2007 — The Bubble Era

Rates were moderate (~6%) but prices had been rising for a decade. The problem was the sheer size of loans buyers needed. When prices corrected sharply in 2008–2009, affordability recovered quickly.

2022–Present — The Double Shock

Both forces hit simultaneously for the first time. Pandemic-era prices surged and never fully corrected. Then mortgage rates went from under 3% to over 7% in under two years. The result: the highest monthly payment burden in the 40-year dataset.

The 2012–2020 era deserves recognition as the mirror image: falling rates and recovering-but-not-overheated prices created the most affordable housing conditions in a generation. That window is now firmly closed.

Finding Three

Today's crisis is not the 2008 bubble — it's structurally different

To demonstrate this, I decomposed the payment burden in each crisis into its two drivers: what would payments be if only prices had changed, and what if only rates had changed?

New Technique: Rolling Correlation

To understand the timing relationship between rate changes and home price changes, I applied a rolling correlation — a technique I learned specifically for this project and had not used before.

New Technique — Learned for This Project

Rolling Correlation with pandas

A rolling correlation computes the Pearson correlation between two series over a sliding window of observations rather than across the full dataset at once. This lets you see how the relationship between two variables changes over time — far more informative than a single static number.

I used pandas' .rolling(8).corr() with an 8-quarter (2-year) window, applied to quarter-over-quarter changes in rates and prices — not the raw levels, which would produce misleading results due to shared upward trends. I also computed 1- and 2-quarter lagged versions to test whether rate changes lead home price changes.

Sources: pandas rolling documentation · Towards Data Science: Four Ways to Quantify Synchrony

Chart 4 — Rolling Correlation: Mortgage Rate Changes vs. Home Price Changes

8-quarter rolling window. Negative values mean rate increases are associated with slower price growth. The 1-quarter lag (orange dashed) being slightly stronger suggests rates lead prices by ~3–6 months.

The rolling correlation reveals the rate–price relationship is not constant. During the 2000s bubble, prices and rates briefly moved together as speculation overwhelmed rate sensitivity. After 2022, the negative correlation strengthened sharply — confirming buyers responded quickly to the rate shock — but prices have remained sticky rather than falling significantly.

Summary

Three findings, forty years of evidence

01
The 3× benchmark is gone

The median home now costs 6–7× the median annual income. This ratio hasn't returned to its historical 3× norm since the early 1990s — and post-crisis floors keep rising.

02
The worst burden on record

Monthly mortgage payments now consume a larger share of income than at any point in this 40-year dataset — worse than the 2008 bubble peak and the high-rate 1980s.

03
A new kind of crisis

Unlike 2008 (price-driven) or the 1980s (rate-driven), today's crisis compounds both simultaneously. Rolling correlation shows rates lead prices by ~1 quarter. Prices are sticky; relief is not automatic.

Methodology

How this analysis was built

All data comes from FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data). The three series were downloaded and merged in Python using pandas in Google Colab. The main wrangling challenge was reconciling three different frequencies: quarterly, annual, and weekly. I resampled the mortgage rate to quarterly averages and used linear interpolation to bring the annual income series to quarterly frequency.

Affordability metrics were built from scratch using the standard mortgage amortization formula (20% down, 30-year fixed). The rolling correlation used pandas' .rolling(8).corr() on quarter-over-quarter changes.

Limitations

National aggregates only — no regional variation. MSPUS tracks new construction only, skewing higher-end. Renters (~35% of households) are absent. The 20% down payment assumption is increasingly unrealistic. Median income conceals within-group inequality.

Reflection

The most surprising finding was that the current crisis is measurably worse than 2008 by the payment-to-income metric. The biggest technical challenge was reconciling three data frequencies — especially deciding between forward-fill and interpolation for the annual income series. Getting the rolling correlation right took time; I initially ran it on raw levels rather than changes, which produced misleadingly strong results.

With more time I'd add city-level Zillow data, include the existing home sale series alongside MSPUS, and model future scenarios: what if rates drop to 5%? What if prices fall 20%?
