The Affordability Gap: How Housing Became Out of Reach

Forty years of data reveal how the American dream of homeownership became financially impossible for millions — and why today's crisis is unlike anything we've seen before.

By Ananya Mehta Data: FRED / Federal Reserve 1984 – 2024

The Story

A benchmark broken for a generation

For decades, financial advisors have offered a simple rule of thumb: a home should cost no more than three times your annual income. It was a reasonable norm for most of the 20th century. Then, quietly at first and then all at once, it stopped being true.

Using data from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, I traced every quarter from 1984 to 2024 — watching home prices, household incomes, and mortgage rates move together and apart. What emerged is a picture of a housing market that has fundamentally changed, not just cyclically but structurally.

"The median U.S. home now costs more than six times the median annual household income — twice what conventional affordability guidelines say it should."

This project asks three questions: How far has the price-to-income ratio drifted from its historical norm? When does the monthly cost of a mortgage become genuinely unaffordable? And is today's crisis the same kind of problem as the 2008 housing bubble — or something structurally different?

Finding One

The 3× rule hasn't held for thirty years

The price-to-income ratio first crossed the 3× benchmark in the early 1990s and has barely looked back. By the mid-2000s it was approaching 5×. After the 2008 crash it pulled back — but only to around 4×, never returning to the historical norm. Then came the pandemic.

Between 2020 and 2022, remote work untethered millions of workers from expensive coastal cities. Demand surged. Supply couldn't keep up. Home prices rose faster in two years than they had in the previous decade. The price-to-income ratio hit its highest point ever recorded in this dataset.

What the decade-by-decade summary shows

The data aggregated by decade makes the structural shift impossible to ignore. In the 1980s and 1990s, the average price-to-income ratio hovered around 3.5–4×. In the 2000s it climbed to 4.5×. In the 2010s, even with post-crash corrections, it averaged above 4×. In the 2020s so far, it is tracking above 6×.

Finding Two

Three eras of unaffordability — each with a different cause

The price-to-income ratio is a useful long-run measure, but it doesn't capture what a buyer actually experiences each month. For that, I calculated the monthly mortgage payment on the median home each quarter, assuming a 20% down payment and a 30-year fixed loan at the prevailing rate. I then measured that payment as a share of monthly income.

Mortgage lenders use 28% as their threshold for "cost-burdened." Above that, conventional wisdom says a household is spending too much of its income on housing.

The dashed red line marks the 28% cost-burden threshold. The shaded area shows the full payment burden each quarter.

What this chart reveals is that there have been three distinct periods of acute housing unaffordability — and each was driven by a different mechanism.

Early 1980s — The Rate Era

Mortgage rates reached 13–14%. Even with relatively modest home prices by modern standards, the monthly payment consumed well over 28% of income. The affordability problem was almost entirely a rate problem.

2005–2007 — The Bubble Era

Rates were moderate — around 6% — but prices had been rising for a decade. The problem was not monthly costs per se, but the sheer size of the loans buyers needed to take on. When prices corrected sharply in 2008–2009, affordability recovered quickly.

2022–Present — The Double Shock

Both forces hit simultaneously for the first time. Pandemic-era prices surged and never fully corrected. Then the Federal Reserve raised rates aggressively to fight inflation, pushing 30-year mortgage rates from under 3% to over 7% in under two years. The result: the highest monthly payment burden in the 40-year dataset.

The 2012–2020 era deserves recognition as the mirror image: falling rates and recovering-but-not-overheated prices created the most affordable housing conditions in a generation. That window is now firmly closed.

Finding Three

Today's crisis is not the 2008 bubble — it's structurally different

When housing prices spiked in the mid-2000s, many observers blamed speculation, lax lending, and a bubble that was bound to pop. And it did. Prices fell sharply after 2008, rates dropped, and affordability recovered within a few years. The crisis was painful but self-correcting.

Today's situation has a different structure. To demonstrate this, I decomposed the payment burden in each crisis into its two components: what would payments be if only prices had changed (holding rates at the pre-crisis level), and what would they be if only rates had changed (holding prices constant)?

The rolling correlation analysis — a technique I learned specifically for this project — adds another layer. On average, rate changes are negatively correlated with home price changes: when rates rise, price growth slows. But this relationship is not constant. During the 2000s bubble, prices and rates briefly moved together as speculation overwhelmed rate sensitivity. After 2022, the negative correlation strengthened sharply — but prices have remained surprisingly sticky rather than falling.

The 1-quarter lag version of the correlation is slightly stronger than the same-quarter version in recent years, suggesting that rate increases take roughly 3–6 months to show up fully in home price data.

Summary

Three findings, forty years of evidence

01
The 3× benchmark is gone

The median home now costs 6–7× the median annual income. This ratio has not returned to its historical 3× norm since the early 1990s — and post-crisis floors keep rising.

02
The worst burden on record

Monthly mortgage payments now consume a larger share of income than at any point in this 40-year dataset — worse than the 2008 bubble peak, worse than the high-rate 1980s.

03
A new kind of crisis

Unlike 2008 (price-driven, self-correcting) or the 1980s (rate-driven, resolved when rates fell), today's crisis compounds both. Prices are sticky; rates remain elevated. Relief is not automatic.

Methodology

How this analysis was built

All data comes from FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data). The three series — median home sale price (MSPUS), real median household income (MEHOINUSA646N), and the 30-year fixed mortgage rate (MORTGAGE30US) — were downloaded and merged in Python using pandas.

The main wrangling challenge was that the three series have different frequencies: quarterly, annual, and weekly respectively. I resampled the mortgage rate to quarterly averages, and used linear interpolation (rather than forward-fill) to bring the annual income series to quarterly frequency.

The affordability metrics were constructed from scratch using the standard mortgage amortization formula, assuming a 20% down payment and 30-year fixed loan. The rolling correlation analysis — a new technique for this project — used pandas' .rolling(8).corr() method with an 8-quarter window applied to quarter-over-quarter changes (not raw levels).

Limitations

This analysis covers national aggregates only — regional variation is entirely invisible in this data. The home price series (MSPUS) tracks new construction only, not the larger resale market, and skews slightly higher-end. Renters, who make up about 35% of U.S. households, are absent from this analysis. The 20% down payment assumption is increasingly unrealistic for first-time buyers. And the median income figure conceals significant within-group inequality.
